After my first year of college, something rearranged.

I had spent a genuinely embarrassing amount of my adolescence wanting a
girlfriend. Not a person — a *girlfriend*, the category, the proof. I would have
told you it was about love. It was mostly about being chosen.

And then at some point it landed, not as a revelation, more like a slow leak:

<Break>
  <Monument size="colossal">I needed God.</Monument>
</Break>

<Pause size="small" />

Which was awkward, because I did not know Him.

## the Indian kid at Mass

I grew up Hindu, in a Hindu house, with a Hindu grandmother who prayed in a way
that was so ordinary and so daily that I did not recognise it as religion until
much later. It was just what the morning was.

And I went to a Catholic school. Mass once a month. Everybody filing in. The
same songs coming back around — the one about giving thanks with a grateful
heart, which I could still hum before I could have told you a single thing about
what it meant.

Here is the part that surprises people: I never once felt like an outsider
there.

I don't say that to be sunny about it. I say it because it is *evidence* about
children. Nobody had told me yet that these were teams. So I sang the song,
because it was a song, and I liked it, and it was Tuesday morning.

<Ledger kind="memory">
Monthly Mass, twelve years old, Indian, Hindu, singing along. Nobody found this
strange, including me.
</Ledger>

<Ledger kind="later">
Somewhere between then and being twenty I acquired a full vocabulary of
divisions — denominations, sects, which things are heresy to which people, who
is allowed to marry whom.
</Ledger>

<Ledger kind="interpretation">
My own reading, and I hold it loosely: God did not hand me those divisions. People did.
I am aware that a person from any of those traditions could tell me why I am wrong,
and might be right.
</Ledger>

## looking, badly

So I started looking. Poorly, at first, the way you look for something when you
don't know its shape.

I read. I asked people questions that were probably rude. I went to things. I
sat in the back. I looked up words. I compared what I was reading to what I had
grown up inside, and found that some of it rhymed and some of it did not, and
that the rhyming parts unsettled me more than the differences did.

I want to say clearly that this was not a rejection of my family's faith. My
grandmother's mornings are still, to me, one of the most credible religious
things I have ever witnessed. I was not running from something. I was walking
toward something I could not yet see.

## two people, one verse

The thing that got its hooks into me early was this:

Two people can read the exact same sentence and come away with two different
lives.

Not because one of them read it wrong. Because a sentence lands in whatever is
already in the room. Your father, your losses, your language, what you were
doing that week.

  <Monument size="large">
    same words.<br />

That could have made me a cynic about the whole enterprise. Instead it did the
opposite. It made the thing feel *alive* — like it was not a document to be
decoded correctly but something that met you where you actually were.

I was, at this point, very deliberately not looking for a relationship. I had
just worked out that a person was not the missing piece.

Which is, of course, exactly when someone showed up.
