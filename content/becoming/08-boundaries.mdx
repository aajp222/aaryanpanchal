I am not going to write this chapter the way that kind of chapter usually gets
written — neither the confession version nor the version that pretends we were
saints.

So, plainly: we made mistakes. There were lines we crossed and then regretted
crossing. There was guilt, real and specific. There were situations where being
alone made a good decision harder to reach, and we kept walking into those
situations anyway and being surprised by what happened in them.

That is the whole account. The details belong to two people and only one of them
is writing this.

## the guilt was information

For a long time I treated guilt as an attack — something to argue with, explain
away, or absorb quietly and carry.

It's not. It's a reading. It is a gauge telling you the load has exceeded what
the structure was designed for. You don't argue with a gauge. You either
strengthen the structure or you reduce the load.

<Ledger kind="feeling">
Guilt. Sharp, and it kept coming back to the same few nights.
</Ledger>

<Ledger kind="interpretation">
At the time: this means I am a bad person and a fraud, especially given what I
say I believe.
</Ledger>

<Ledger kind="later">
It meant a limit had been crossed, which is a different statement about a
different subject. The first is about identity. The second is about a decision.
Only one of them can be acted on.
</Ledger>

## what an engineer knows about limits

Here is the thing I had, professionally, understood for years and had never once
applied to my own life.

A constraint is not a reduction of a system. A constraint is frequently *the
reason the system works.* A beam is only useful because it is fixed at the ends.
A spring only does its job inside a defined travel. Take the boundaries off a
mechanism and you don't get a freer mechanism, you get a pile of parts and a
noise.

<Break>
  <Monument size="huge">
    A limit is not the enemy of the thing.<br />
    Sometimes it is <em className="italic">what makes the thing possible.</em>
  </Monument>
</Break>

We got better at this, late. Later in the relationship there were more
boundaries and they were ours, chosen rather than imposed, and — this surprised
me — the thing got *warmer*, not colder. It felt less like restraint and more
like having somewhere to stand.

I want to flag that for later, because it is going to matter. There is a night
coming, months after everything ends, where I get invited somewhere and I know
exactly what road that is, and I say no.

I could not have done that without this chapter. That refusal is not willpower.
It is a limit I had already learned the value of, arriving in time to be useful.
