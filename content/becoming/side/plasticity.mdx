Three ways a material answers a load.

**Elastic.** It deforms, and when you take the load off it returns exactly to
where it was. No memory. Nothing retained. As far as the material is concerned,
nothing happened.

**Brittle.** It holds, and holds, and holds, and gives no indication of distress,
and then it fails completely and all at once with no warning at all. Glass does
this. Cast iron does this. Very hard things do this.

**Plastic.** It deforms past the point of return, and it *stays* deformed, and it
goes on carrying the load in its new shape.

## the thing that took me years to notice

In every class I have ever sat in, plasticity is taught as a kind of failure.
Permanent deformation. The part is out of spec. It will not go back.

And I sat in those classes as a person who had spent fifteen years trying to be
brittle on purpose. Hard. Unmoved. Returns to original position, no memory of the
load.

## what carries over

I want to be careful, because engineering metaphors about feelings can get
extremely corny extremely fast, and most of them do not survive contact with an
actual engineer.

But this one holds, because the mechanism is the same, not just the vocabulary.

- A constraint is often what makes a system work, not what limits it.
- Failure is data. It is the most information-dense event available, and the only
  reason it feels like a verdict is that we take it personally.
- You cannot optimise something you refuse to measure honestly.
- Every real design is a set of tradeoffs. There is no configuration where you
  win on every axis, and looking for one is how you end up shipping nothing.
- Iteration beats planning past a certain complexity. You have to build the bad
  version to find out what the question actually was.

I learned all of that at a bench with parts that did not work, and then found out
it was all also true about a person, which was annoying, because I would have
preferred to keep those two things separate.
