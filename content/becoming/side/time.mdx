Both of my parents lost their fathers young.

I know this the way you know facts about your parents — as background, as
something that was always true, mentioned occasionally and never dwelt on. It
took me an unreasonably long time to convert it from a fact into an image.

Two people, roughly the age I am now, in a country they had not yet left,
absorbing the largest loss available, and then getting up and going to work and
eventually getting on a plane and starting over somewhere else.

And then never really talking about it, because that generation didn't.

## the fear, stated plainly

Here is mine, and I do not think it is unusual, I just think people don't say it
out loud:

I do not want to outlive everyone I love.

Not *I am afraid to die* — that one is abstract and I can put it down. This one
is specific. I am afraid of the version where I am the last one holding all of
it. Where every story has only one witness left and no one to check it against.

<Ledger kind="feeling">
A low-grade dread that arrives, reliably, on the drive back after visiting
family.
</Ledger>

<Ledger kind="fact">
Nothing about tomorrow is promised to me, to them, or to anyone I have ever met.
</Ledger>

<Ledger kind="interpretation">
For a while this made me want to grip harder. Call more, record more, extract
more, as though sufficient documentation would help.
</Ledger>

<Ledger kind="later">
It doesn't. The only actual response to impermanence is attention. Not more
holding — better noticing.
</Ledger>

## why this is not a morbid chapter

Because the conclusion isn't dread. It's this:

The ordinary day is the whole thing. Not the milestone, not the graduation, not
the photograph. The Tuesday. The unremarkable phone call where nothing gets
discussed. The meal where everyone is on their phone a bit and it's fine.

Those are not the filler between the important parts. Those are what there is.
