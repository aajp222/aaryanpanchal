The family conflict came back almost immediately. Of course it did. It had never
gone anywhere; we had simply stopped standing in front of it for a few weeks.

And then came the argument. The real one.

## both sides

I am going to write this as fairly as I know how, which means writing her side
first and writing it properly, not as a strawman I then get to knock down.

<LedgerSplit>
  <Ledger kind="interpretation" label="What she was saying">
    That as long as we were near each other, boundaries were not going to hold.
    That we had proof of that — a night of psalms is still a phone call at four
    in the morning. That continuing was going to keep hurting both of us and
    everyone attached to us, and that wanting something is not the same as
    something being good for you.
  </Ledger>
  <Ledger kind="interpretation" label="What I was saying">
    That it was not fair to hand me the entire responsibility for ending
    something that neither of us wanted to end. That being asked to be the one
    who does it — alone, and then to carry having done it — was a weight I did
    not agree to and did not think I deserved.
  </Ledger>
</LedgerSplit>

Both of those are reasonable. That is the whole problem. I spent a long time
trying to find the flaw in hers so that I could be the wronged party, and it
isn't there.

## the sentence

And then she said the thing that ended it, and it was not cruel, and it is the
sentence I have thought about more than any other in this entire story.

<Break>
  <Monument size="huge">
    &ldquo;because I can&rsquo;t<br />
    do it <em className="italic">myself.</em>&rdquo;
  </Monument>
</Break>

<Pause size="small" />

I was furious when I heard it. I want that on the record, because I am not
interested in a version of this where I was gracious in the moment. I was not. I
heard it as *do my hard thing for me*.

<Ledger kind="anger">
Why is this mine to carry. Why do I have to be the one who does it and then
also be the one who has done it. You want the outcome without the cost of it.
</Ledger>

<Ledger kind="later">
That is not what she said. She said she could not do it. Not that she would not
— that she could not. Which is an admission of how much it would cost her, made
out loud, to the person it would cost her the most to admit it to. I heard
weakness being offloaded. Looking at it now I think it was probably the most
honest sentence either of us said that day.
</Ledger>

I still don't know if I'm right about that. I've held both readings for a long
time now and I have made my peace with holding both.

## the small deletions

Then came the part nobody prepares you for, which is not the conversation. It's
the admin.

A nickname changed back to a name. A song removed from a playlist because it had
become unusable. The particular small digital rituals that two people build
without noticing — the reactions, the shorthand, the thing you always sent —
switched off one at a time, deliberately, by hand.

That is where it actually happened, for me. Not in the argument. In the
unbuilding.

And then, at the end, we did the thing properly. Apologies, both directions.
Thank you. I love you. Goodbye.

## and then

<Break>
  <Monument size="huge">
    And then I <em className="italic">cried.</em>
  </Monument>
</Break>

Which — you have to understand what that meant.

I had not really cried since I was seven years old. That was the entire
architecture. That was the sentence I said to my father and then spent fifteen
years validating.

And it just went.

I did not decide to. There was no moment of surrender where I nobly allowed
myself to feel something. It simply stopped holding, the way a thing stops
holding when the load finally exceeds what it was built for.

And lying there, wrecked, at twenty-one years old, the sentence that arrived was
one I had said in Hindi to my father when I was seven.

*Mera dil patthar ho gaya.*

Except it clearly, obviously, demonstrably had not.
